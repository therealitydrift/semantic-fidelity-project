# Applied Semantic Fidelity

This folder contains applied papers from the Semantic Fidelity Project examining how meaning can be lost, distorted, or overextended as information moves through AI systems.

The papers focus on specific points of failure rather than redefining the broader framework. Together, they examine abstraction, summarization, fluent reconstruction, retrieval, citation, and evaluation as distinct stages where a representation can remain coherent, useful, and apparently well-supported while losing important relationships contained in its source.

## Papers

### 01 — How Abstraction Causes Information to Lose Meaning

Examines how selection, compression, normalization, recontextualization, reconstruction, and recursive compression can alter meaning while preserving a representation’s surface coherence.

### 02 — Why an AI Summary Can Be Factually Accurate and Still Misleading

Shows how a summary can contain individually true statements while changing the conclusion, confidence, scope, or practical implication a reader takes from the source.

### 03 — Why ChatGPT Can Sound Right While Getting the Answer Wrong

Examines fluent reconstruction and synthetic coherence, especially the movement from source evidence to inference, generalization, and recommendation.

### 04 — Why Retrieval and Citations Can Still Lose the Meaning of a Source

Explores the difference between retrieving a source and allowing that source to constrain an answer. Introduces the distinction between **source presence** and **source force**.

### 05 — How to Evaluate Semantic Fidelity Across an AI Workflow

Provides a diagnostic method for tracing meaning across source material, retrieval, intermediate representations, generated outputs, agents, and downstream reuse.

## Shared Framework

Across the series, semantic fidelity refers to how well meaning survives transformation.

The papers distinguish semantic fidelity from narrower measures such as factual accuracy, citation presence, retrieval success, similarity, and task completion. A representation can perform well on those measures while still changing relationships that govern what the source permits a reader or system to conclude.

The series also connects these local failures to Reality Drift. A single fidelity failure is not itself Reality Drift. The broader condition becomes relevant when derivative representations increasingly organize action while the underlying sources or conditions lose their ability to correct them.
